\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Stroke transport modeling}
Recent studies collectively highlight the difficulty of a universal stroke routing protocol given the diversity of patients and hospital capabilities. Schlemm \emph{et al.}~\cite{schlemm2017} and Al Fatah \emph{et al.}~\cite{alfatah2018} propose strategic routing models, using prehospital severity scales and agent-based simulation to weigh stroke severity against transport times, and Maas \emph{et al.}~\cite{maas2020} stress region-specific pathway design, contrasting drip-and-ship with mothership. Holodinsky \emph{et al.}~\cite{holodinsky2018} model the probability of a good outcome as a function of onset-to-treatment time for each subtype under both pathways and map the better pathway for a region; their outcome functions are the reward model used here. The randomized RACECAT trial~\cite{racecat2022} found that direct transport of suspected LVO patients to a thrombectomy center was not superior to transport to the nearest local center across a whole region, which is the clinical motivation for a patient-specific policy rather than a fixed bypass rule. These models answer the question for a region or a pathway; we answer it for an individual patient at dispatch time, with the subtype uncertainty and the transfer stage inside one formulation.

\subsection{Operations research for stroke networks and EMS}
Ardestani-Jaafari and Kucukyazici~\cite{ardestani2022} optimize inter-facility transfer protocols for a regional stroke network, choosing network-level rules offline and evaluating them for Quebec. Their object is the protocol; ours is the per-patient dispatch decision, solved online, and the two are complementary: a protocol of their class could be used as a comparator in our framework, and our policy could be the dispatch layer beneath their transfer rules. Zachrison \emph{et al.}~\cite{zachrison2026} take a decision-analytic approach to the same per-patient destination question, simulating 400,000 patient-location scenarios in the northeastern United States with hospital-specific treatment times; their model chooses between the two consensus pathways for a given patient, whereas ours searches over every reachable hospital and the subsequent transfer, and is distilled for use without computation. More broadly, MDPs and approximate dynamic programming have been applied to ambulance redeployment and dispatch~\cite{maxwell2010}, where the state is the fleet rather than the patient. Capacity-aware models of receiving centers (queueing for angiography suites and beds) exist in the operations literature; we do not model capacity (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) and treat the spread of patients across hospitals as a qualitative indication only.

\subsection{Interpretable policies for deployment}
A computed policy is only deployable in an ambulance if the crew can apply and explain it. Policy distillation into decision trees~\cite{bastani2018} trains a tree on the actions of a stronger policy and verifies the tree against it; clinical decision rules have long taken the same form. Our tree is trained on the MDP's own labels, evaluated against the MDP on patients never used for training, and its depth is chosen by a sweep that trades leaves against the share of the MDP's gain that the tree recovers.

\subsection{What this paper adds}
Relative to the stroke-transport models, we provide a per-patient policy that handles subtype uncertainty and transfer within a single formulation, and evaluate it with replicates, stratification by neighborhood income and urbanicity, and sensitivity analyses. Relative to the operations literature, the policy is online, interpretable and measured for latency. Relative to the clinical literature, hospital capability is defined by verified certification with a sensitivity analysis for uncertified thrombectomy sites, and in-hospital intervals are taken from current registry benchmarks rather than assumed.

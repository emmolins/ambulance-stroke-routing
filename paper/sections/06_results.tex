\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Primary comparison: San Francisco Bay Area}
Across 10 replicates of 1,000 patients, the MDP policy raised the mean probability of an excellent outcome from 0.357 under nearest-hospital routing to 0.379 (paired difference 0.023, 95\% CI 0.022--0.024, $p < 10^{-6}$ after Bonferroni correction, $d_z = 0.73$; Table~\ref{tab:main}) and the median from 0.343 to 0.406 (Fig.~\ref{fig:hist}). The realized rewards give the same picture: mean 0.357 against 0.379, difference 0.022 (95\% CI 0.021--0.023). A spike in the nearest-hospital distribution at 0.327, which holds 32\% of patients, reflects those who reach every treatment outside the window in which earlier arrival adds benefit; routing cannot help them, and under the MDP the share at that value falls to 23\%. The largest single-patient gain was a 30\% relative increase.

Against the heuristics, nearest-hospital routing underperformed every alternative. The MDP policy outperformed the nearest-stroke-center rule by 0.019 (95\% CI 0.018--0.020) and the nearest-EVT-center rule by 0.0006 (95\% CI 0.0006--0.0006): the two policies agree for 71\% of patients, and where they differ the MDP is never worse (1,009 patients better, none worse). The small margin over the nearest-EVT-center rule hides an operational difference. Of the 2,875 patients on whom the two policies diverged, 1,866 had identical predicted outcomes, yet the nearest-EVT-center rule drove them a mean 16.2 minutes farther (26.0 against 9.8 minutes first-leg travel, up to 64 minutes more), and overall it concentrated transports on 10 hospitals against 51 under the MDP (the two busiest EVT centers received 52\% of its patients, against 35\% under the MDP). These detours fall on patients for whom earlier arrival does not change the outcome, such as hemorrhages and late-presenting ischemic strokes, where the reward function has flattened. Because the reward does not penalize travel that leaves the outcome unchanged, it understates the operational cost of a fixed bypass rule; the travel-time tie-break in the planner is what lets the MDP avoid it.

\subsection{Geographic and equity analysis}
To locate where routing matters most, we simulated five patients in each cell of a 200$\times$200 grid over the Bay Area (13,917 populated cells) and mapped the mean outcome under each policy (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}). Relative to nearest-hospital routing, the MDP improved the cell mean by a median of 4.7\%, by more than 10\% in 3,788 cells (27\%), by more than 25\% in 613 cells, and by a maximum of 73\%; the 90th percentile of the cell-level improvement was 19\%. The largest gains lie in the East Bay corridor from Hayward through Fremont to Livermore, in the North Bay and along the Peninsula coast, which lie farther from EVT-capable centers; San Francisco, Palo Alto and San Jose, with proximate EVT-capable centers, changed little. Cells where the MDP cell mean was below the nearest-hospital mean (13.6\% of cells, none by more than 0.03) are scattered rather than regional \pending{[check with MJK: these are the 7.8\% of patients whose recorded first-destination reward is below nearest-hospital's; the depth-2 value sums first- and second-hop rewards, so the planner can prefer a first destination with lower immediate reward]}, and against the nearest-EVT-center rule the MDP was below the heuristic in a single cell. Stratified by census-tract income quartile and urbanicity (Table~\ref{tab:equity}), the MDP's gain over nearest-hospital routing was 0.020 to 0.021 in every income quartile and 0.020 in urban against 0.019 in rural tracts, so the benefit is not concentrated in or absent from any stratum. Against the nearest-EVT-center heuristic the gain was 0.0017 in the lowest income quartile against 0.0005 in the highest, and 0.0023 in rural against 0.0009 in urban tracts: the advantage of the MDP over the bypass rule, though small, is largest where incomes are lowest and where the nearest EVT-capable center is farthest.

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}
Table~\ref{tab:sens} reports the MDP-minus-nearest difference under perturbed travel times and alternative in-hospital intervals. The difference is unchanged to three decimals under $\sigma = 0.10$ and $0.20$ travel-time noise (0.022 in both cases) and moves from 0.023 to 0.021 as travel times are scaled from $\times 0.7$ to $\times 1.3$, because a perturbation applied to every first leg moves both policies' arrival times in the same direction; the margin over the nearest-EVT-center rule stays between 0.0004 and 0.0007 throughout. It depends strongly on door-in-door-out time: with $T_{\text{DIDO}}$ at 60 minutes the gain over nearest-hospital routing is 0.008, at 90 minutes 0.015, at the registry median of 121 minutes 0.022 and at the upper quartile of 175 minutes 0.028. A longer door-in-door-out interval penalizes drip-and-ship and favors direct transport to an EVT-capable center, which is what the MDP chooses for most patients; the result is consistent with the registry association between DIDO and outcome~\cite{royan2026} and explains why a single 60-minute in-hospital interval, as assumed in earlier work, understates the policy's value. Treating the four uncertified thrombectomy sites as EVT-capable (15 centers) and re-planning leaves the gain at 0.022 (95\% CI 0.021--0.023 over 3 replicates) and the margin over the nearest-EVT-center rule at 0.0004.

\subsection{Decision-tree policy}
The depth sweep (Table~\ref{tab:tree}) shows that a single split recovers 99.4\% of the MDP's gain over nearest-hospital routing on 200 held-out patients, and that trees beyond depth 4 add leaves without gain. The depth-1 rule is: if time since onset is at most 188 minutes, go to the nearest EVT-capable center; otherwise go to the nearest thrombolysis-capable center. Beyond about 180 minutes, 90 minutes of door-to-puncture places EVT after the 270-minute end of the outcome window whatever the travel time, so only earlier thrombolysis can still change the outcome; the learned threshold of 188 minutes is that boundary. Depth 2 adds one qualification for late presenters: go to the EVT-capable center anyway if it is no farther than the thrombolysis-capable one (Fig.~\ref{fig:tree}); depth 3 adds the non-stroke-center branch for patients for whom it is the closest option by a margin. The shallowest tree within 1\% of the MDP is the depth-1 rule, and the depth-2 tree is the one we would print on a card. On the training cohort the MDP sent 74\% of patients to an EVT-capable center, 23\% to a thrombolysis-capable center and 4\% to a non-stroke-center hospital, and in 55\% of cases the destination was not the closest hospital. The nearest-EVT-center rule itself recovers 97.7\% of the gain on the same held-out patients, which is why the tree's first split reproduces it and its second split corrects it for late presenters.

\subsection{Real-time feasibility}
A depth-2 decision, including the routing-engine call for the patient's travel-time row, took a median of 16~ms (95th percentile 24~ms, maximum 32~ms); depth 3 and 4 took 25 and 49~ms. Every value is three orders of magnitude inside the dispatch window, so the chosen depth is set by the model (one transfer) rather than by compute, and the decision tree needs no computation at all.

\subsection{Rhode Island}
In Rhode Island the MDP and the state's nearest-EVT-center protocol gave nearly identical mean outcomes (0.379 against 0.378; difference 0.0007, 95\% CI 0.0006--0.0007), and both outperformed nearest-hospital routing by 0.020 (95\% CI 0.019--0.021) and 0.019 (Table~\ref{tab:ri}); the nearest-stroke-center rule coincides with nearest-hospital routing because every Rhode Island hospital is at least a thrombolysis-capable center. The protocol sends every patient to Rhode Island Hospital; the MDP sent 71\% there and the rest to eight other hospitals, with a mean first-leg travel of 18 minutes against 24, and where the two diverged (2,921 patients) the outcome was identical for 1,879 and better under the MDP for the remainder. In a small state with one EVT center the optimal policy largely coincides with the centralized protocol, and the value of the MDP lies in sparing the detour, and the single center's load, for patients who do not benefit from it.

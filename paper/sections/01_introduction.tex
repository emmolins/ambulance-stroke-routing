\section{Introduction}

\jama{JAMA para 1, lightly edited. Keep: the two-protocol contrast (CA default-nearest vs RI single-CSC) is the motivation for a computed policy.}
Ambulance routing for suspected stroke is a time-critical decision made under uncertainty, and protocols vary considerably across the United States. In parts of California the absence of a coordinated routing framework means patients are often transported to the nearest hospital by default, which can delay access to definitive care. Rhode Island offers a contrasting case study in severity-based routing, directing suspected large-vessel-occlusion patients to a single comprehensive stroke centre, which has been shown to shorten times to treatment~\cite{jayaraman2023}. That approach is not practical in larger or denser regions, where travel distances grow and a single centre would be overwhelmed. \new{Between these two fixed rules lies a decision that can be computed: given where the patient is, how long since onset, and what each reachable hospital can do, which destination maximises the expected outcome?}

\jama{JAMA para 2, edited: capability-based tier names replace CSC/PSC/clinic (Reviewer 2).}
Approximately 1.9 million neurons are lost for every minute treatment is delayed~\cite{saver2006}. Delivering the right treatment is complicated by the range of stroke subtypes a patient may have: large-vessel occlusion (LVO), non-LVO ischaemic stroke (nLVO), and intracerebral haemorrhage, each requiring different intervention, and a share of positive prehospital screens are stroke mimics. LVO is treated with endovascular thrombectomy (EVT), available only at EVT-capable centres, and with intravenous thrombolysis; nLVO is treated with thrombolysis alone. \new{We therefore classify hospitals by on-site capability: EVT-capable centres (certified Comprehensive or Thrombectomy-Capable Stroke Centers), thrombolysis-capable centres (Primary and Acute Stroke Ready hospitals), and acute-care hospitals without stroke certification.} The subtype is unknown at pickup and becomes known only after imaging on arrival, so the destination choice is a decision under uncertainty whose consequences include a possible second journey.

Points: (1) this is a sequential decision problem with a well-defined state, action set and objective, not a clinical judgement call; (2) the quantities it depends on are available in real time to a dispatch system (GPS position, onset time from the caller, road travel times from a routing engine, a hospital capability table); (3) the barrier to deployment is not data but a policy that is computable within the dispatch window and explainable to the crew applying it; (4) hence the two artefacts of this paper: an MDP policy and its decision-tree distillation.]}
